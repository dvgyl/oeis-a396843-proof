## Source and scope

[OEIS A389542](https://oeis.org/A389542), accessed on 2026-10-24, defines the
normalized generating function

\[
A(x)=x+2x^2+10x^3+64x^4+446x^5+\cdots
\]

by the identity

\[
A\bigl(A(x)^2-x^2\bigr)=4A(x)^3.
\tag{1}
\]

The entry has offset 1 and attributes the sequence to Paul D. Hanna, dated
2025-11-01. Its comment conjectures the following statement for every integer
\(n>1\):

\[
a(n)\equiv
\begin{cases}
2\pmod4,&n=2^k+1\text{ for some }k\ge0,\\
0\pmod4,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\tag{2}
\]

The results below prove (2) for the unique normalized integral solution of (1).
In particular, the formal construction is connected to the literal OEIS
defining equation and is proved unique. No existence or identification
hypothesis is left to the reader.

The entry still labeled (2) a conjecture on the access date. Searches for the
identifier with “proof” and “congruence,” and for the defining equation, did not
locate a prior proof. This limited search does not establish that the statement
was globally open, that this proof is the first, or that unpublished proofs do
not exist. The other formulas and numerical real evaluations listed in the
entry are outside the scope of this result.

## 1. An integral construction

Construct a formal series \(L\in\mathbb Z[[x]]\), with constant coefficient zero
and coefficient of \(x\) equal to one, by

\[
L=x+L^2-2xL(4x^3).
\tag{3}
\]

This construction requires no division. If \(L=\sum_{n\ge1}\ell_nx^n\), then
\(\ell_1=1\), and for \(n\ge2\),

\[
\ell_n=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\ell_i\ell_{n-i}
-\begin{cases}
2\,4^{(n-1)/3}\ell_{(n-1)/3},&3\mid n-1,\\
0,&3\nmid n-1.
\end{cases}
\tag{4}
\]

Every coefficient on the right has index less than \(n\). Thus (4) determines a
unique integral series. The same construction works over every commutative
ring, which also shows that it commutes with reduction of coefficients.

The Lean proof gives this argument in terms of formal powers of \(X\): if two
normalized inputs agree below degree \(n\), their images under the right side
of (3) agree below degree \(n+1\). A coefficientwise recursion then constructs
the unique fixed point.

Set

\[
R(x)=x-2xL(x).
\tag{5}
\]

Then

\[
\begin{aligned}
R(x)^2+R(4x^3)
&=x^2+4x^2\bigl(-L+L^2+x-2xL(4x^3)\bigr)\\
&=x^2,
\end{aligned}
\tag{6}
\]

by (3). Since \(R=x+O(x^2)\), it has a unique compositional inverse
\(A=x+O(x^2)\) over \(\mathbb Z[[x]]\).

Compose (6) on the right with \(A\). Because \(R(A(x))=x\), this gives

\[
x^2+R(4A(x)^3)=A(x)^2.
\]

Apply \(A\) to the identity \(R(4A^3)=A^2-x^2\). The two inverse identities
give exactly (1).

## 2. Uniqueness of the integral source series

Conversely, let a normalized integral series \(A\) satisfy (1), and let \(B\)
be its compositional inverse. Substituting \(x=B(t)\) in (1) gives

\[
A\bigl(t^2-B(t)^2\bigr)=4t^3.
\]

Applying \(B\) shows

\[
B(t)^2+B(4t^3)=t^2.
\tag{7}
\]

There is at most one normalized integral solution of (7). Indeed, suppose two
such solutions \(G,H\) first differ in degree \(r\ge2\), with

\[
G-H=cx^r+O(x^{r+1}),\qquad c\ne0.
\]

Because \(G+H=2x+O(x^2)\),

\[
G^2-H^2=2cx^{r+1}+O(x^{r+2}).
\]

Meanwhile, \(G(4x^3)-H(4x^3)\) has order at least \(3r\ge r+2\). Subtracting
the two instances of (7) therefore gives \(2c=0\), impossible in
\(\mathbb Z\). Hence \(B=R\), and consequently the original \(A\) is exactly
the inverse constructed above.

This proves existence and uniqueness of the normalized integral series in the
source equation before any modular argument is used.

## 3. The auxiliary series modulo two

Reducing (3) modulo two yields

\[
\overline L=x+\overline L^{\,2}=x+\overline L(x^2)
\quad\text{in }\mathbb F_2[[x]].
\tag{8}
\]

The second equality is the characteristic-two squaring identity for formal
power series. Comparing coefficients, the odd degrees greater than one have
coefficient zero, and the coefficient in degree \(2m\) equals that in degree
\(m\). Since the coefficient in degree one is one, repeated division of an
even degree by two gives

\[
[x^n]\overline L=
\begin{cases}
1,&n=2^k\text{ for some }k\ge0,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\tag{9}
\]

Equivalently, \(\overline L=\sum_{k\ge0}x^{2^k}\), interpreted purely as a
formal series. Each coefficient receives only finitely many contributions.

## 4. The inverse is an involution modulo four

Work now in \((\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z)[[x]]\), writing \(L,R\) for the reductions
of the integral series. Formula (5) gives \(R\equiv x\pmod2\). It follows that

\[
L(R(x))\equiv L(x)\pmod2,
\]

so multiplying by two gives the equality

\[
2L(R(x))=2L(x)\pmod4.
\tag{10}
\]

Consequently,

\[
\begin{aligned}
R(R(x))
&=R(x)-2R(x)L(R(x))\\
&=R(x)-2R(x)L(x)\\
&=(x-2xL)(1-2L)\\
&=x-4xL+4xL^2\\
&=x\pmod4.
\end{aligned}
\tag{11}
\]

Thus \(R\) is its own compositional inverse modulo four. Coefficient reduction
commutes with compositional inversion for normalized formal series. Since
\(A=R^{\langle-1\rangle}\) over the integers, uniqueness of inverses implies

\[
A(x)\equiv R(x)=x-2xL(x)\pmod4.
\tag{12}
\]

For \(n>1\), (12) gives \(a(n)\equiv-2\ell_{n-1}\pmod4\). By (9),
\(\ell_{n-1}\) is odd precisely when \(n-1=2^k\). The residues \(-2\) and
\(2\) are equal modulo four, so this proves (2).

## Independent finite verification

Run from the repository root:

```sh
python3 scripts/verify_square_reversion.py --degree 80
```

The script uses exact, unbounded Python integers. One computation solves the
original source equation directly, checking every division by two. A second
constructs \(L\) by (4), then \(R\) by (5), and reverts \(R\) coefficient by
coefficient. These two independently specified recursions agree through degree
80. The computed series matches all 22 coefficients displayed on the OEIS page.

It also checks the literal source equation and the inverse equation through
degree 81, both compositional inverse identities through degree 80, the
auxiliary equation, the parity support in (9), and every predicted residue for
\(2\le n\le80\). In this range, the residue-two indices are
\(2,3,5,9,17,33,65\).

The machine-readable result is
[`validation/square_reversion_numeric.json`](../validation/square_reversion_numeric.json).
These finite checks guard against transcription and source-identification
errors. The Lean theorems establish the claims for all indices.

To rebuild the formal proof with the pinned Lean and Mathlib versions:

```sh
cd lean
lake build Round3.SquareReversion
```
